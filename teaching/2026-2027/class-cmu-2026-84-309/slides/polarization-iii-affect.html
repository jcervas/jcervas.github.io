1. Their Figure 1, with a movable end year

The article was written in 2018 and its series ends in 2016. Leave the slider there and you see what the authors saw, including the dip they were careful to flag. Then drag it to 2024.

Turning leaners off drops everyone who says “independent, closer to the Democrats” from the count. Every year comes in several degrees higher, because leaners are the least hostile partisans in the file. The authors count them; so does the default here.

3. What a mean of 20 is hiding

Every partisan in 2024, sorted into ten-degree bins. The out-party distribution is not a bell curve around a cool average; it is a pile against the floor.

4. How cold is cold?

The share of partisans who put the other party at the very bottom of the scale — and, on the second row of buttons, the share who put a party above 50, the neutral point the question itself defines. Neither asks you to believe that the distance from 40 to 30 means the same as the distance from 10 to 0, which is what a mean assumes. This is not in the article, which reports means.

5. Who is hostile?

The same series split four ways. Notice that the bands move together: the gap between youngest and oldest is about as wide at the start as at the end, which is not what a story about one cohort replacing another would look like.

6. Does sorting explain it?

The article says sorting accounts for “only about 5% of the variance” in affective polarization (p. 142). Split the country by how sorted people are, on Mason’s own score from last session, and judge for yourself.

Both halves are true and the session says so. The least sorted got much more hostile while their sorting barely moved — so sorting cannot be what moved the country. And the distance between the most and least sorted grew, on either cut — so “about 5% of the variance” does not describe these data either. The two numbers answer different questions: theirs is about explaining the change over time, this is about predicting the level within a year.

7. Party feeling spills into everything else

Eight feeling thermometers from our own survey. Not one of these questions mentions a party. Bars are this class’s Democrats; the dots are the country, split by party, on the four groups the 2024 ANES also asks about. The room has five Republicans, so the mirror image has to come from the national data — and the evangelical row is where you can see it.

Bars: this class, Democrats and Democratic leaners only — five Republicans is too few to report. Dots: ANES 2024, which asks four of the eight; labor unions and Christian fundamentalists are near matches for our union members and evangelicals rather than the same question.

8. Your party wins, the economy improves

The most reproducible claim in the article (p. 138, after Bartels). The ANES has asked since 1980 whether the nation’s economy got better or worse; this is the net — percent saying better minus percent saying worse — by the respondent’s own party, with Republican presidencies shaded. Watch what the two lines do at each boundary.

No account in which people simply report the economy accurately produces two lines that swap places whenever the White House does. This class, asked the same question in August 2026 under a Republican administration, is in the readout above.

9. Does disliking them make you vote?

The article’s claim on p. 139, after Iyengar & Krupnikov: dislike of the other party drives people to participate. Self-reported turnout, split by how affectively polarized the respondent is, terciles cut within each year.

Believe this one least. People who care enough about politics to dislike the other side are the same people who turn out, and nothing here separates those two. The zigzag is midterms.
