Guessing race from a name and an address

Bayesian Improved Surname Geocoding (BISG) estimates the probability that a person belongs to each racial and ethnic group using only their surname and where they live. Courts, regulators, and researchers rely on it when records leave race out. Type a name below, add more information, and watch each piece of evidence change the prediction.

Step by step: how the evidence adds up

BISG is Bayes’ rule applied over and over. Each piece of evidence multiplies every group’s current probability by a factor: how much more (or less) common that evidence is among members of the group than among people overall. The results are then rescaled to add up to 100%. Switch a step off to see what it contributes.

How BISG works

BISG (Elliott et al. 2009) combines two facts that are each only partly informative. Some surnames are held mostly by members of one group: the 2020 Census counted about 531,000 people named Nguyen, and 95% of them identified as Asian, for example. And neighborhoods in the United States remain racially segregated, so where someone lives also carries information. Bayes’ rule combines the two, assuming that, within a racial group, surname and location are unrelated.

P(race | surname, place) ∝ P(race | surname) × P(place | race)
P(place | race) = (residents of the place in that race) ÷ (all U.S. residents of that race)

The BIFSG extension (Voicu 2018) multiplies in a first-name term the same way, P(first name | race). This tool also lets you add a middle name and sex, following the fuller model in Imai & Khanna (2016). With every input switched on, the full calculation is:

P(race | all evidence) ∝ P(race | surname)
  × P(first | race) × P(middle | race)
  × P(sex | race) × P(place | race, sex)

Each multiplier shown in the step-by-step panel is a group’s new probability divided by its old one. A multiplier of ×3 means the new evidence is three times as common among that group as among people overall, given everything learned so far.

Where the numbers come from

  • Last names: U.S. Census Bureau, Frequently Occurring Last Names in the 2020 Census by Race and Hispanic Origin. It covers 156,621 last names held by 100 or more people, with a count of people in each group. P(race | last name) is each group’s share of the people with that name. Names not on the list get the Bureau’s “All other names” row. Methods: Census Brief C2020BR-14.
  • First and middle names: U.S. Census Bureau, Frequently Occurring First Names in the 2020 Census by Race and Hispanic Origin, covering 53,615 first names. P(first name | race) is the number of people in a group with that name divided by everyone in that group. An unlisted name uses the “All other names” row, which is informative because rare first names are more common in some groups than others. The Census publishes no middle-name list, so middle names are scored against the first-name list. That is a simplification. Methods: Census Brief C2020BR-13. Both name lists are on the Bureau’s 2020 names page.
  • Neighborhoods and sex: 2020 Census Demographic and Housing Characteristics File, tables P12H–P12O (sex by race and Hispanic origin), retrieved from the Census API for every state, county, tract, block group, and ZIP Code Tabulation Area in the 50 states and D.C. Puerto Rico is not included.
  • ZIP codes: the Census Bureau does not tabulate USPS ZIP codes, which are mail routes. It tabulates ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs), blocks grouped by the ZIP code most of their addresses use. A ZIP code that serves only post office boxes or one large building has no ZCTA. When you enter only a ZIP code, the state and county shown are the ones holding most of its residents, estimated from the Bureau’s 2020 ZCTA-to-tract relationship file (each tract’s population split by shared land area).
  • Address lookup: the Census Geocoder, using the current address benchmark and 2020 census geography. It returns the address’s census block and its ZCTA.

Every input comes from the 2020 Census. The six groups match the Census name files: non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic Asian or Pacific Islander, non-Hispanic American Indian or Alaska Native, non-Hispanic multiracial, and Hispanic of any race. People who chose only “Some other race” (and are not Hispanic) are left out of the neighborhood counts too, because the name files have no matching category. The starting point (step 0) is each group’s share of the 2020 U.S. population on these definitions.

Things to notice and cautions

  • It predicts probabilities, not identities. A 70% White prediction means that among many people with the same inputs, roughly 7 in 10 would identify as White. It does not tell you how any one of them identifies. Applications either add up the probabilities across many people (the more defensible use) or assign each person to the most likely group (simple, but it throws information away).
  • It works best for groups that are segregated or have distinctive names. It is accurate for Hispanic and Asian surnames and for Black residents of segregated neighborhoods. It is poor at identifying American Indian and multiracial people, whose names and neighborhoods are rarely distinctive.
  • Zeros are absolute. If a block group recorded no residents of a group, or a surname shows 0% for a group, Bayes’ rule rules that group out entirely, however strong the other evidence is.
  • Smaller areas are noisier. Starting in 2020 the Census Bureau adds deliberate statistical noise (differential privacy) to protect respondents. The noise matters most for small areas and rare names.
  • People move, marry, and change their names. The data describe the country on April 1, 2020. Women who took a spouse’s surname are a well-known source of error.
  • The independence assumption is wrong at the margins. A Black Garcia and a Hispanic Garcia may not live in the same kinds of places, but BISG assumes they do.

Questions for discussion

  1. Enter your own name. Is the prediction right? If not, which input misled it, and why?
  2. Try Lee, Smith, or Jackson with addresses in different cities. Which input moves the answer more for these names, the surname or the neighborhood?
  3. Find a case where switching from tract to block group changes the most likely group. Should we trust the smaller area more?
  4. Many companies know only a customer’s ZIP code. Enter a name with just a ZIP, then with a full address in that ZIP. How much does the prediction change, and for whom would a ZIP-only estimate be most misleading?
  5. A regulator uses BISG to check whether a lender charges minority borrowers more. Should it add up probabilities or assign each borrower to one group? What could go wrong with each?
  6. Voting rights cases use BISG to estimate the racial makeup of a voter file. Whose votes are most likely to be miscounted, and in which direction?
